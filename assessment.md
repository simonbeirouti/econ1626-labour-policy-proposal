Overview
You will write a concise, evidence-based policy proposal advising a specific government department or agency on how to respond to AI's impacts on the labour market. the agency can be a local, state, Commonwealth or any foreign government. You must anchor your proposal in a specific, real, current policy context — for example, an active government inquiry, a Productivity Commission or Treasury review, a concrete contemporary debate about AI in the jurisdiction of your choice, a jobs/skills strategy, or a comparable OECD/international process. 

Your brief should analyse likely effects on employment, tasks, wages, and industrial structure; evaluate policy options against explicit criteria; and recommend a clear, actionable, costed package (e.g., reskilling, labour-market adjustment, competition/data policy interfaces).

Part One: Policy proposal
1. Choose a real policy client and context: Name a specific department/agency (e.g., "Deputy Secretary, Employment & Skills, Department of Employment and Workplace Relations") and a specific current policy process your brief is feeding into (an inquiry, a strategy under development, a review, a contemporary debate). State this in one sentence at the top of your brief.
2. Define the problem: the channels through which AI is changing work (task automation/augmentation, changes to wages and job design, industry restructuring), grounded in your chosen context.
3. Assess the impacts on the workforce — employment, tasks, wages, industrial structure — including a distributional/equity lens (e.g. effects across regions, occupations, age groups, or employment types).
4. Develop at least 3 concrete policy options. For each, specify the mechanism (what it actually does), who it targets, and its rough scale/cost. Evaluate the options against explicit criteria (e.g. efficiency, equity, feasibility, fiscal cost).
5. Make a recommendation: a clear package with an implementation plan (timeline, responsibilities, costs, KPIs).

Your analysis should draw on credible, checkable sources appropriate to a policy brief — for example:

- Labour force and occupational data (e.g. ABS Labour Force Survey, ABS occupational/industry data)
- AI exposure/task-automation research (e.g. OECD, ILO, or peer-reviewed studies on AI and occupational exposure)
- Australian policy analysis (e.g. Productivity Commission, Treasury, Jobs and Skills Australia)
- International comparators where relevant (e.g. OECD Employment Outlook, comparable country case studies)

Generic news commentary or AI-generated summaries are not sufficient as primary evidence — use them, if at all, only to point you toward primary sources.

Part Two: AI Use Reflection (200 words)
In a separate file, `reflection.md` (excluded from the 1,200-word proposal count), briefly and honestly describe:

- Which AI tools you used, and for what (e.g. drafting/structuring, Markdown/Git help, editing, checking references) — distinguish this clearly from any use in your research or argument-building.
- At least one specific instance where you accepted, rejected, or substantially edited AI output, and why.
- One limitation, challenge, or risk you noticed when using AI tools during this assessment.

This is assessed on specificity and insight, not on how much or how little AI you used.

Assessment Format and Structure (Markdown)
Use clear headings and concise paragraphs: 
- Title & Executive Summary (120–150 words) 
- Problem Definition & Context (what’s changing and why) 
- Analysis of Impacts (employment/tasks/wages/industry; distributional/equity lens) 
- Policy Options (≥3) with criteria-based evaluation 
- Recommended Package & Implementation Plan (timeline, responsibilities, costs, KPIs) 
- Risks & Mitigations 
- References 

You may include a simple table for your options-by-criteria matrix
