# AI and work in Australia: policy proposal scoping outline

**Status:** research and option design for [Assessment 2](../assessment.md), not the 1,200-word submission. The assignment requires **one** named client and live policy process in the final brief. The five portfolios below are alternatives and potential partners, not five separate proposals.

## Common assessment framework

AI affects *tasks* before it affects whole occupations. For each example below, check (1) employment and entry-level opportunities, (2) tasks and job quality, (3) wages or bargaining power, and (4) whether work shifts toward large providers, small firms or new specialist roles. Treat the impacts below as plausible channels to test, not measured outcomes. [JSA's AI Capacity Study](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study) finds uneven exposure and early adoption; its [labour-market analysis](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study/our-gen-ai-transition-labour-market-dynamism) reports no clear evidence yet of declining entry-level roles attributable to generative AI. [DEWR's July 2026 report](https://www.dewr.gov.au/download/17666/ai-and-employment-australia/43205/ai-and-employment-australia/pdf) found employment grew 5.6% in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations versus 9.5% in the least-exposed fifth between November 2022 and February 2026. DEWR stresses that this does **not** prove AI caused the difference: routine clerical roles were already declining, and the statistical result is sensitive to model choices. The report does not measure wages, productivity or job quality. The [ABS business survey](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/technology-and-innovation/characteristics-australian-business/2024-25) found 12% of businesses reported AI use in 2024–25, with use varying by size and industry.

**Ratings:** Efficiency = likely value relative to resources; equity = whether vulnerable workers benefit; feasibility = ability to deliver under current institutions. H/M/L are preliminary judgments, not measured scores. Fiscal cost is stated separately. Every A$ figure below is an **illustrative one-year pilot assumption**, not an announced program, budget estimate or verified unit cost. It excludes procurement work and the cost of scaling nationally unless stated. Firm, state and worker costs may arise in addition to the Commonwealth amount.

## 1. Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR)

**Live context:** DEWR's [2026–27 corporate plan](https://www.dewr.gov.au/download/17793/department-employment-and-workplace-relations-corporate-plan-2026-2027/43700/department-employment-and-workplace-relations-corporate-plan-2026-2027/pdf) names the new **AI Employment and Workplaces Forum** as its tripartite process for technology's workplace impacts. Its [July 2026 AI and employment report](https://www.dewr.gov.au/download/17666/ai-and-employment-australia/43205/ai-and-employment-australia/pdf) provides a monitoring framework. This is the strongest fit for an economy-wide labour proposal.

**Three example problems and workforce channels**

1. **Routine cognitive tasks shrink in clerical, customer service and junior professional roles.** Drafting, sorting and summarising may take less staff time. Employment could fall in some entry routes or shift toward review and client work; wage effects depend on productivity, demand and bargaining. Test by occupation, age and region rather than assuming losses.
2. **Workers bear the cost of moving into changed roles.** Training time and foregone earnings can block mobility, especially for casual workers, mature workers and people outside major cities. New AI supervision and domain-specialist tasks may grow while existing credentials lose value.
3. **Algorithmic management can make work harder to contest.** Automated rostering, performance scores and work allocation may increase output but also create unsafe targets or biased decisions. This can weaken autonomy and bargaining, particularly for low-paid or platform workers. The [National AI Plan](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/national-ai-plan/spread-benefits) identifies these workplace risks and the role of consultation.

| Candidate option | Mechanism and target | Illustrative Commonwealth cost | Efficiency / equity / feasibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Paid transition training** | DEWR funds short, accredited or employer-verified training plus paid release time for workers in roles with verified task changes. Pilot: 2,000 workers, priority to low-income and regional applicants. | 2,000 × (A$2,000 training + A$1,500 wage replacement) + A$1m administration = **A$8m**. | **M / H / M.** Mobility and future earnings may rise, but courses must connect to real vacancies; paid time supports people unable to train after work. |
| **B. Workplace AI consultation and review pilot** | Grants to 100 employers and worker representatives for task mapping, consultation, audit of algorithmic management, and a human review route for high-stakes work decisions. | 100 × A$50,000 + A$2m evaluation = **A$7m**. Employer staff time extra. | **M / H / M.** Could prevent poor job design; works only if representatives can inspect systems and decisions can be changed. |
| **C. AI labour-market observatory** | Expand JSA/DEWR monitoring of vacancies, occupations, wages, adoption and regional differences, with public quarterly indicators and a comparison design for pilots. | 20 specialist staff × A$180,000 fully loaded + A$1.4m data/evaluation = **A$5m**. | **H / M / H.** Better targeting and early warning at modest cost; information alone does not compensate affected workers. |

**Most useful brief question:** Which combination of paid adjustment support and worker decision rights should DEWR test through its existing tripartite process? Assess against current programs to avoid funding training that employers would have provided anyway.

## 2. Department of Health, Disability and Ageing

**Live context:** The department published a [Safe and Responsible AI in Health Care legislation and regulation review](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/health-technologies-and-digital-health/what-we-do/artificial-intelligence-ai-in-health-care). The TGA regulates some diagnostic and treatment AI; the department has also developed clinical-use guidance. Health service delivery and much of the workforce sit with states, territories and providers, so any Commonwealth pilot needs partners.

**Three example problems and workforce channels**

1. **Clinical documentation and triage tools change clinicians' task mix.** Less typing could release care time, but checking incorrect notes may add work. Demand for clinical verification and informatics roles may rise; measured time saved may not become shorter shifts or higher pay.
2. **Uneven model quality can shift risk onto clinicians and patients.** A clinician may be accountable for an opaque suggestion, while a provider captures savings. Errors could concentrate in poorly represented populations or under-resourced settings, including rural services.
3. **Automation can alter entry-level learning.** Junior clinicians and administrative staff may do fewer routine cases that teach judgment. Workforce skill formation and supervision costs matter even if immediate output rises.

| Candidate option | Mechanism and target | Illustrative Commonwealth cost | Efficiency / equity / feasibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Clinician-led workflow pilots** | Ten services compare AI-supported documentation with usual practice; fund training, protected evaluation time and human sign-off. Target high-administration care teams, with rural sites included. | 10 × A$600,000 + A$2m independent evaluation = **A$8m**. State/provider staff time extra. | **M / M / M.** Could free care time; savings depend on integration and error-checking burden. |
| **B. Shared safety and subgroup testing** | Commission independent evaluation protocols for tools used in clinical work, including error rates across patient groups and a staff incident-reporting route. | 5 tool classes × A$1m + A$1m coordination = **A$6m**. | **H / H / M.** Shared evidence has spillover benefits and can detect unequal harm; access to vendors' systems may be hard. |
| **C. Clinical AI supervision training** | Train supervisors in when to rely on tools, how to document overrides and how to preserve junior learning. Target 5,000 clinicians. | 5,000 × A$800 + A$1m curriculum/evaluation = **A$5m**. | **M / M / H.** Relatively easy to deliver; training alone will not fix unsafe tools or staffing shortages. |

## 3. Department of Education

**Live context:** Education Ministers endorsed the [reviewed Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools](https://www.education.gov.au/schooling/resources/australian-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-schools) in June 2025. The [National Teacher Workforce Action Plan](https://www.education.gov.au/national-teacher-workforce-action-plan) remains active, including workload work. School employment is mainly a state, territory and sector responsibility, which limits direct Commonwealth control.

**Three example problems and workforce channels**

1. **AI may reduce lesson-planning and administrative time but increase verification.** Teachers could spend more time on student support, or workloads could rise if AI creates extra feedback and monitoring expectations. Retention and working hours are key outcomes.
2. **Assessment and curriculum work must change.** More AI-generated student work increases the cost of checking learning. New teacher skills in assessment design and AI literacy become valuable; contractors and education technology vendors may gain a larger role.
3. **Access differs across schools.** Better-resourced schools may obtain safer tools and training first, potentially widening student outcomes and teacher workload gaps, especially in remote schools.

| Candidate option | Mechanism and target | Illustrative Commonwealth cost | Efficiency / equity / feasibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Teacher workload pilots** | Co-fund 100 schools to test AI for low-stakes administration, with teacher choice, privacy checks and measured hours saved. Prioritise hard-to-staff schools. | 100 × A$60,000 + A$2m evaluation = **A$8m**. State/sector contributions extra. | **M / H / M.** May improve retention if time savings are real and protected; delivery needs jurisdiction agreements. |
| **B. National assessment exemplars** | Fund teacher teams to design and publish subject-specific assessment tasks and moderation guidance that work in an AI-rich classroom. | 20 subject/level teams × A$150,000 + A$1m dissemination = **A$4m**. | **H / M / H.** Shared material avoids duplicated effort; uptake varies by school and curriculum. |
| **C. Rural teacher AI support** | Deliver training and technical support to 2,000 rural/remote teachers, with paid release time and accessible support. | 2,000 × A$1,500 + A$1m delivery = **A$4m**. | **M / H / M.** Addresses an access gap; connectivity and staffing constraints remain. |

## 4. Services Australia

**Live context:** Its [Automation and AI Strategy 2025–27](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-05/automation-and-ai-strategy-2025-27.pdf) and [transparency statement](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/automation-and-artificial-intelligence-transparency-statement?context=22) cover service delivery, workforce skills and human oversight. The agency says it does **not** use AI to process claims, and final compliance or decision actions have human oversight. The problems below concern support tools and possible future use, not an existing AI claim-decision system.

**Three example problems and workforce channels**

1. **Assistive tools change frontline service work.** Search, drafting and feedback triage may reduce routine handling time, while complex cases require more experienced staff. Measure both throughput and customer outcomes, not staff reductions alone.
2. **Automation errors can be costly to vulnerable clients and frontline staff.** Incorrect flags, explanations or routing can generate rework and distress. Staff need time and authority to override tools; appeal and audit tasks may expand.
3. **Digital-first channels can leave some users behind.** Regional clients, people with disability and people with limited digital access may need human assistance even as routine interactions move online. Staffing pressure could shift toward complex service centres.

| Candidate option | Mechanism and target | Illustrative Commonwealth cost | Efficiency / equity / feasibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Frontline assistive-tool trial** | Controlled trial of retrieval/drafting support for 1,000 service staff; no automated claim decisions. Compare handling time, accuracy, rework and staff workload. | 1,000 × A$2,000 training/licensing + A$3m integration/evaluation = **A$5m**. | **M / M / H.** Could improve service quality; benefit depends on system accuracy and integration. |
| **B. Human review and override capacity** | Create specialist review time and tracked overrides for AI-assisted routing, fraud or compliance signals. Prioritise high-impact cases and complaints. | 50 staff × A$150,000 fully loaded + A$1.5m audit = **A$9m**. | **M / H / H.** Reduces error costs and improves contestability; ongoing staff cost is material. |
| **C. Assisted-access guarantee** | Preserve 20 staffed sites or outreach teams in places with weak digital access while testing new digital channels. | 20 × A$400,000 = **A$8m**. | **L–M / H / M.** Strong access protection; targeting and recurrent funding need care. |

## 5. Department of Industry, Science and Resources (DISR)

**Live context:** DISR is implementing the [National AI Plan](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/national-ai-plan), including the National AI Centre's adoption work and expectations for AI infrastructure. The [Productivity Commission's final data and digital report](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries-and-research/five-productivity-inquiries/meeting-productivity-challenge/) is a relevant policy comparator. DISR has an economic-development mandate; a labour proposal would need DEWR/JSA as partners.

**Three example problems and workforce channels**

1. **Small firms may lack skills to adopt useful AI.** Workers in large firms may gain productivity sooner, while SMEs lose competitiveness or buy poor tools. The issue is diffusion and complementary training, not merely access to a model.
2. **AI infrastructure creates jobs and local costs.** Data centres need trades and specialist operations staff, but power and water costs can fall on local communities. Construction jobs can be temporary; lasting skilled roles require training pathways.
3. **Vendor concentration can affect local firms and wages.** Dependence on a few model, cloud or software suppliers may shift returns away from Australian adopters and workers. Yet domestic ownership alone would not guarantee higher wages.

| Candidate option | Mechanism and target | Illustrative Commonwealth cost | Efficiency / equity / feasibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. SME adoption and worker training vouchers** | Through the National AI Centre, co-fund task redesign and worker training for 1,000 SMEs; require a worker consultation and outcome plan. | 1,000 × A$8,000 + A$2m delivery/evaluation = **A$10m**. SMEs contribute time and any extra tool costs. | **M / M / H.** Could spread productivity gains; grants risk paying firms that would adopt anyway. |
| **B. Infrastructure skills agreements** | Negotiate apprenticeships and local training places with data-centre projects, supported by a pilot training fund. Target electricians, technicians and regional entrants. | 500 places × A$10,000 + A$1m administration = **A$6m**. Employers fund wages and supervision. | **M / M–H / M.** Links investment to workforce capability; depends on real project demand. |
| **C. Open procurement and interoperability trial** | Publish model portability and switching requirements for 20 public AI procurements, with supplier-neutral testing. | 20 × A$150,000 technical/legal work + A$1m evaluation = **A$4m**. Suppliers may face compliance costs. | **M / M / M.** May reduce lock-in and improve entry, but requirements could increase procurement costs. |

## Selection for the 1,200-word proposal

**Recommended client:** Department of Employment and Workplace Relations, **AI Employment and Workplaces Forum**. Its current name appears in the department's 2026–27 corporate plan. A more precise individual addressee can be confirmed before submission.

**One-sentence context for the top of the brief:** “This brief advises the Department of Employment and Workplace Relations' AI Employment and Workplaces Forum on a 2027 pilot response to the labour-market risks identified in DEWR's 2026 *AI and employment in Australia* monitoring framework and the National AI Plan.”

**Narrow problem:** How should Australia help workers move from shrinking routine cognitive tasks into higher-value work while protecting their say over AI-assisted workplace decisions? This has a direct labour-market link, an active policy process, credible Australian evidence and at least three options with different mechanisms.

**Provisional package:** Trial option A (paid training) and option B (consultation/review) together; fund option C (monitoring) to test both. The illustrative first-year Commonwealth envelope is **A$20m** (= A$8m + A$7m + A$5m), subject to real costings and overlap with existing programs. This is a pilot proposal, not an official budget request.

**Implementation sketch:** Months 1–3: DEWR/JSA and employer/union representatives choose occupations, eligibility and pilot sites using current workforce data; months 4–5: recruit employers and workers, record baseline pay, tasks, hours and vacancies; months 6–10: deliver training and workplace pilots; months 11–12: compare participants with matched non-participants and decide whether to expand, redesign or stop. Report employment retention, changes in earnings and hours, internal redeployment, worker-reported autonomy, contested algorithmic decisions and cost per sustained job or wage gain. A later earnings follow-up would require a separately costed extension. Compare with a genuine status quo and record employer costs.

**Risks to test:** deadweight spending on training that would happen anyway; selection bias if only motivated firms join; consultation reduced to a formality; productivity gains captured without wage or workload improvements. Eligibility, a comparison group, worker surveys and publishable evaluation criteria address these risks.

## Evidence to gather before drafting `proposal.md`

1. Use the [ABS Labour Force survey](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/labour/employment-and-unemployment/labour-force-australia) and JSA occupation/region tables to choose two or three occupations and quantify their size and wage baseline. Do not infer actual job loss from exposure scores.
2. Use the full [DEWR 2026 report](https://www.dewr.gov.au/download/17666/ai-and-employment-australia/43205/ai-and-employment-australia/pdf) carefully: cite its tentative relative employment signal and robustness limits, and do not attribute the difference to AI without further evidence.
3. Check existing Commonwealth training and workplace programs to refine eligibility, administrative route and genuine incremental cost.
4. Verify the legal authority and current status of any proposed information, consultation or review right before describing it as existing law.
5. Replace every pilot unit-cost assumption with a quoted program cost, a comparable Australian program, or an explicit transparent estimate in the final brief.
