# Rational next steps for five Australian AI and work portfolios

**Working analysis for Assessment 2, 27 September 2026.** This builds on [policy-scoping.md](policy-scoping.md). It compares the most defensible *sequence* of actions for each portfolio. It does not select the client or answer for the final 1,200-word brief. The scoping document's dollar figures are illustrative pilot assumptions, not government costings.

## Decision rule

Start with a use case and a measurable public outcome. Use existing powers and programs where they already fit. Fund a larger intervention only if evidence shows a material problem, the proposed mechanism addresses it, and benefits exceed public and private costs. For AI at work, measure changes in **employment, tasks, wages, hours and service quality** rather than equating AI exposure with job loss. [DEWR's 2026 monitoring report](https://www.dewr.gov.au/download/17666/ai-and-employment-australia/43205/ai-and-employment-australia/pdf) finds a tentative slowdown in more exposed occupations, but does not establish that AI caused it. [JSA's capacity study](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study) finds more scope for augmentation than whole-job replacement.

## 1. Employment and Workplace Relations: prepare workers and improve workplace decisions

**Why this department acts:** DEWR is responsible for employment, skills and workplace relations. Its [2026–27 corporate plan](https://www.dewr.gov.au/download/17793/department-employment-and-workplace-relations-corporate-plan-2026-2027/43700/department-employment-and-workplace-relations-corporate-plan-2026-2027/pdf) places AI's workplace impacts with the new AI Employment and Workplaces Forum.

1. **Extend the evidence base first.** DEWR and JSA should add wage, hours, vacancy, entry-level and job-quality measures to the existing occupation monitoring framework. Link these, where possible, to observed employer adoption so that changes in exposed occupations are less easily confused with older trends.
2. **Test workplace decision rights in a narrow setting.** Through the Forum, select a few uses of algorithmic rostering, performance assessment or work allocation. Ask employers and worker representatives to document the rule, affected workers, error rate, consultation process and route to human review. Use existing workplace and safety obligations as the starting point; identify any genuine legal gap before recommending new law.
3. **Target paid training where a transition is observed.** Offer short, paid, vacancy-linked training to workers whose tasks have changed and whose employer cannot redeploy them. Compare subsequent employment and earnings with similar eligible workers who did not receive the offer. Expand only if outcomes beat the existing training pathway after accounting for costs and deadweight spending.

**Public value and test:** Better job mobility and fairer decisions could spread productivity gains without blocking useful adoption. The decisive evidence is sustained earnings, redeployment, time in work, and fewer substantiated decision errors per dollar spent. The A$20 million combined pilot in [the scoping outline](policy-scoping.md#selection-for-the-1200-word-proposal) is a hypothesis to cost properly, not an immediate national program.

## 2. Health, Disability and Ageing: prove care benefits and contain clinical risk

**Why this department acts:** Its [AI in health care review](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-07/safe-and-responsible-artificial-intelligence-in-health-care-legislation-and-regulation-review-final-report.pdf) found that departmental legislation can largely accommodate AI, while evidence of benefits remains limited. The TGA already regulates some medical-device AI, and the department has issued [clinical-use guidance](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/health-technologies-and-digital-health/what-we-do/artificial-intelligence-ai-in-health-care).

1. **Choose one low-risk workflow and measure its net effect.** Start with documentation support in willing services. Measure clinician time *including checking and correction*, note quality, patient experience and time available for care. Include rural and diverse patient groups in evaluation.
2. **Apply proportionate assurance to higher-stakes tools.** For diagnostic or triage uses, require local validation, subgroup performance checks, incident reporting and a named clinician accountable for the final decision. Coordinate with TGA and state regulators so a new Commonwealth process does not duplicate existing controls.
3. **Protect the training pipeline.** Track whether junior clinicians lose routine cases that build judgment; reserve supervised case review and teach staff when to override AI. Scale tools only if care quality and staff workload both improve.

**Public value and test:** The gain sought is safer care and more clinician time with patients, especially in underserved areas. The test is net time saved, error rates across patient groups, staff burnout and care outcomes relative to ordinary practice. A time-saving claim alone is insufficient.

## 3. Education: learn from current workload pilots before expanding

**Why this department acts:** Education Ministers have an [AI in Schools framework](https://www.education.gov.au/schooling/resources/australian-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-schools). The Commonwealth's [Workload Reduction Fund](https://www.education.gov.au/workload-reduction-pilots) already supports 27 state and territory pilots, including generative AI projects, with Phase 2 expected to finish during 2026–27. States, territories and school sectors employ teachers and run schools.

1. **Evaluate the AI-related workload pilots against comparable schools.** Count actual teacher hours, correction time, student learning and retention intentions. Publish where tools worked, where they added work and the conditions needed for success.
2. **Share reusable assessment and procurement guidance.** With jurisdictions and teachers, publish examples of assessment that reveal student learning when AI is available, plus minimum privacy and vendor terms. Reuse the existing framework rather than commissioning another national statement.
3. **Direct support to schools with weak implementation capacity.** Offer training and technical help to rural and low-resource schools if the pilot evidence shows a gap. Avoid a universal tool subsidy before measuring unequal access and benefit.

**Public value and test:** Successful policy would protect teacher time and student learning while reducing differences between schools. The relevant measures are workload hours, retention, student outcomes, safe access and cost per hour genuinely saved.

## 4. Services Australia: improve assistance while preserving review and access

**Why this agency acts:** Its [Automation and AI Strategy 2025–27](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-05/automation-and-ai-strategy-2025-27.pdf) emphasises trust, staff scrutiny and explainable decisions. The agency's [transparency statement](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/automation-and-artificial-intelligence-transparency-statement?context=22) says it does not use AI to process claims and that final compliance and decision actions have human oversight. The [Commonwealth policy for responsible AI use](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/ai-use-case-impact-assessment) already requires impact assessment and monitoring for in-scope uses.

1. **Trial staff assistance on a bounded, reversible task.** Start with information retrieval or drafting support, not claim determination. Compare accuracy, handling time, rework, staff workload and customer comprehension with a control group.
2. **Make human oversight effective.** Give staff authority and time to reject a poor recommendation. Log overrides, complaints and outcomes; review them for group-specific errors. Complete the required AI use-case impact assessment before deployment and reassess after material system changes.
3. **Protect non-digital routes where needed.** Monitor whether people with disability, limited digital access or complex cases can still reach effective human help. Retain or add assisted access in locations where a digital change worsens outcomes.

**Public value and test:** Faster routine service is valuable only if people receive correct, contestable and accessible decisions. Measure resolution time, error and appeal rates, customer access, staff workload and cost per resolved case.

## 5. Industry, Science and Resources: spread useful adoption without subsidising weak projects

**Why this department acts:** DISR implements the [National AI Plan](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/national-ai-plan). Its [AI Adopt program and National AI Centre](https://www.industry.gov.au/science-technology-and-innovation/technology/artificial-intelligence) already support smaller firms, so a new voucher scheme must add value beyond existing support.

1. **Evaluate existing adoption support.** Track whether participating SMEs redesign tasks, train workers and gain productivity compared with similar non-participants. Separate genuine additional adoption from firms that would have bought tools anyway.
2. **Target the actual constraint.** If evaluation shows skills are the barrier, fund worker time and practical training. If vendor switching or poor interoperability is the barrier, test procurement and portability rules. If neither is binding, avoid another subsidy.
3. **Tie infrastructure support to verifiable local benefits and costs.** For data centres, assess grid and water impacts, workforce demand, apprenticeships and durable local jobs before attaching concessions or training funds. Coordinate with energy and state authorities so costs are not shifted silently to households.

**Public value and test:** Australia benefits when smaller firms can compete and workers share productivity gains, while communities are compensated for infrastructure costs. Measure productivity, worker earnings, firm entry, switching costs, local jobs and net public cost.

## What makes sense for Australian society overall

The portfolios should share a small set of comparable outcomes and publish evaluations, while each retains responsibility for its own decisions. DEWR and JSA can coordinate labour indicators; sector departments can test whether AI improves their actual services; the Digital Transformation Agency can provide the common public-sector risk process. This avoids treating every AI use as the same policy problem.

The order of action should be **observe, test, evaluate, then scale**. Where a wrong decision can seriously harm someone, such as clinical care or an income-support outcome, scrutiny and an effective human review route are needed before deployment. Where harm is more reversible, such as a drafting assistant, a controlled trial can reveal benefits and costs faster. Across both cases, publish who gains, who pays and whether productivity gains reach workers and the public.

## Which department offers the strongest assessment topic?

DEWR still offers the clearest direct fit with the assignment's employment, tasks, wages and industry criteria. Services Australia is a strong alternative if the proposal focuses on a bounded public-sector workforce and service-delivery problem. Education has an especially concrete existing program to evaluate. The final brief should choose **one** of these clients and one decision, rather than combine all five portfolios into a 1,200-word report.
