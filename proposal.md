# AI and the Australian workforce: DEWR policy proposal outline

**Policy client and live context:** Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR), advising its AI Employment and Workplaces Forum after DEWR's 2026 labour-market monitoring report and the National AI Plan.

## Executive Summary

- Open with the policy question: how should DEWR help workers adapt to changing tasks while keeping workplace AI decisions open to scrutiny?
- State the evidence carefully. DEWR found slower employment growth in more AI-exposed occupations after 2022, but its analysis does not establish that AI caused the difference.
- Explain the two problems the proposal addresses: workers can face training costs when their roles change, and algorithmic work allocation or performance systems can be hard to challenge.
- Preview three options: paid transition training; worker consultation and human review; stronger labour-market monitoring.
- Provisional recommendation: test consultation and monitoring first, then release training places where there is evidence of changed tasks and suitable vacancies.
- Give the indicative ceiling of A$17 million over 18 months. Say that this is a planning estimate and that expansion depends on measured employment, earnings and job-quality gains.

## Problem Definition and Context

- Use the [Acemoglu–Restrepo task framework](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.33.2.3): AI may take over some tasks, make workers more productive in others, and create new work. A task's exposure is not a job-loss forecast.
- Anchor the brief in [DEWR's 2026 report](https://www.dewr.gov.au/download/17666/ai-and-employment-australia/43205/ai-and-employment-australia/pdf). Employment grew 5.6% in the most exposed fifth of occupations and 9.5% in the least exposed fifth from November 2022 to February 2026. The report warns that the association is sensitive to modelling choices and predates AI in some clerical roles.
- Name the current decision route: DEWR's [2026–27 corporate plan](https://www.dewr.gov.au/download/17793/department-employment-and-workplace-relations-corporate-plan-2026-2027/43700/department-employment-and-workplace-relations-corporate-plan-2026-2027/pdf) identifies the AI Employment and Workplaces Forum. The [National AI Plan](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/national-ai-plan/spread-benefits) supports worker training and consultation.
- **Research for final draft:** choose two or three occupations from JSA's exposure data; add their ABS employment and earnings baselines. Check what training and consultation programs already cover them.

## Analysis of Impacts

- **Employment and tasks:** clerical, customer-service and junior professional roles may do less routine drafting or sorting and more checking, exception handling and client work. [JSA](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study/our-gen-ai-transition-labour-market-dynamism) reports no clear evidence yet of a broad decline in entry-level roles caused by generative AI.
- **Productivity:** cite [Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's customer-support study](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044) as evidence that assistance can help novice workers. Mark it clearly as US firm-level evidence, not an Australian estimate.
- **Wages and job design:** gains may reach workers as pay or less stressful work, but could instead become profit, lower prices or tighter targets. DEWR's report does not measure wages or work intensity. These are outcomes to investigate.
- **Industrial structure:** larger firms may have more capacity to purchase tools and redesign workflows; smaller suppliers may lose work or find new markets. Treat this as a hypothesis until Australian adoption and firm data support it.
- **Equity:** examine differences by occupation, age, gender, region and employment type. Test whether low-paid and casual workers can afford unpaid training. Do not assume all groups face the same effect.
- **Research for final draft:** add occupation-level wages and employment, regional comparisons and evidence on actual employer adoption. Distinguish AI effects from older clerical trends and the business cycle.

## Policy Options

- **Option A — paid transition training.** DEWR funds short courses and paid time for up to 2,000 workers whose tasks have changed and who have a realistic destination vacancy. *Indicative cost:* 2,000 × (A$2,000 course + A$1,500 income support) + A$1 million administration = **A$8 million**. Efficiency: medium if courses lead to better jobs; equity: high because time is paid; feasibility: medium because places and employers are needed. Check for spending on training that would happen anyway.
- **Option B — workplace consultation and review.** Support 100 employers and worker representatives to map AI-affected tasks, inspect consequential work decisions and create a documented human review route. *Indicative cost:* 100 × A$50,000 + A$2 million evaluation = **A$7 million**, plus employer staff time. Efficiency: medium; equity: high for affected workers; feasibility: medium because representatives need usable information and authority to act.
- **Option C — extend DEWR/JSA monitoring.** Add observed AI adoption, wages, hours, vacancies, entry-level hiring and job-quality measures to DEWR's existing framework. *Indicative cost:* five specialist staff × A$180,000 × 1.5 years + A$650,000 data work = **A$2 million**. Efficiency: high for targeting; equity: medium because information itself does not compensate workers; feasibility: high if it extends current work.
- **For final draft:** benchmark each unit cost against an Australian program or supplier quote. Count employer and administrative costs separately. Compare each option with the status quo and with other ways to achieve the same outcome.

## Recommended Package and Implementation Plan

- **Provisional package:** begin with B and C (A$9 million). Reserve A$8 million for A, released only if DEWR/JSA finds verified task changes and suitable vacancies. Maximum illustrative Commonwealth cost: **A$17 million over 18 months**; no automatic national rollout.
- **Months 1–3:** DEWR convenes the Forum; JSA selects occupations and comparison groups; employers and unions agree on data access, review procedures and baseline measures.
- **Months 4–6:** recruit workplaces and workers; record tasks, pay, hours, AI adoption and existing training. Publish the evaluation plan before the pilot starts.
- **Months 7–15:** run consultation and review pilots. Start training cohorts only where the evidence gate is met.
- **Month 18:** independent evaluator reports results and advises whether to stop, redesign or expand. A longer earnings follow-up would need separate funding.
- **KPIs:** employment retention, redeployment, real hourly earnings, paid hours, training completion, worker-reported autonomy, review requests and corrected decisions; report cost per sustained improvement.

## Risks and Mitigations

- **Attribution:** exposure is not adoption. Use adoption records, several exposure measures and comparable non-participants before claiming an AI effect.
- **Deadweight spending:** require evidence that funded training fills a gap and, where appropriate, an employer contribution.
- **Token consultation:** give worker representatives enough information and time to question a system; record employer responses and reversals.
- **Unequal access:** monitor take-up and results across regions, age groups and employment types; change eligibility if the intended workers are not reached.
- **Research for final draft:** verify existing legal consultation and review rights before proposing a new duty.
